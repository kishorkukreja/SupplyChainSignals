# Pricing Analyst Research: Cost Structure & Price Transmission
## Soybean Oil + Sugar Deficit → Festival-Goods Margin Compression (India, 2026)

**Mandate:** Cost structure, price transmission history, elasticity, segment differentiation, substitution economics, and a sensitivity framing for the working thesis on India's 2026 festival season.

**Analyst stance up front:** The thesis is directionally supported and, on the sugar side, arrives with unusually good timing — a genuine 2025-26 production shortfall is compressing stocks right into festival demand, which is a sharper and more current story than the "soybean oil price/supply pressure" framing, where the live evidence is more about *rupee-driven import cost* and *currency/duty policy* than a clean global supply shock. The organized-vs-unorganized asymmetry is well evidenced. Treat the "soybean oil supply pressure" leg of the thesis as needing a rewrite toward "edible-oil import-cost pressure via rupee depreciation + duty policy," not a soybean-specific supply shock.

## 1. Cost Structure Findings

- **FACT** — In biscuit manufacturing, raw materials typically account for 65-75% of operating expenditure, and within that, palm oil alone can account for nearly a fifth of raw-material costs. [Value Research Online summary, Aug 2025, via search snippet]
- **FACT** — For savory snack/namkeen makers such as Bikaji, Prataap Snacks and Gopal Snacks, frying oils make up nearly a quarter to a third of total raw-material costs. Snack makers see a more immediate margin lift when frying-oil prices fall, versus bakery businesses where contracts/recipes reset more slowly. [Value Research Online, Aug 2025, referenced via search — https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/226086/edible-oil-industry-set-for-boom/ (site blocked to direct fetch; content via search synthesis only — treat as secondary-sourced)]
- **FACT** — For Indian biscuit manufacturers, flour, sugar, edible oils and cocoa directly drive gross margins; Britannia reported raw-material costs rising ~15% YoY in the June 2025 (Q1 FY26) quarter. [search synthesis, referencing Britannia Q1 FY26 commentary, mid-2025]
- **FACT** — ITC's FMCG-Foods-relevant consolidated gross margin contracted ~700 basis points to 52.1% in Q1 FY26 (June 2025 quarter) YoY, driven by sharp commodity inflation in edible oil, wheat, potato and leaf tobacco (leaf tobacco is cigarettes-segment, not Foods, but reported at the consolidated/segment level alongside Foods commentary). [Investing.com/search synthesis of ITC Q1 FY26 results commentary, ~July-Aug 2025]
- **FACT** — Britannia's commodity basket (LPG, CNG, sugar, palm oil) remained elevated through FY26; management said it had offset only about half of input inflation via price increases, the rest unabsorbed, partly because ~50-55% of Britannia's sales sit at ₹5/₹10 price points where price hikes are hard to push through quickly. [Britannia FY26 earnings call commentary, synthesized from Investing.com transcript search result, 2025-2026]
- **FACT** — Britannia stated it buys palm oil forward and was "well covered for another five months" as of its FY26 earnings commentary — i.e., organized players use forward/hedged procurement that unorganized players cannot access. [Britannia FY26 earnings call, via search synthesis]
- **INFERENCE (assumption-based, not a disclosed number)** — No company in this search disclosed an exact "sugar as % of COGS" figure for biscuits/snacks specifically (sugar is usually bundled into a generic "commodity basket" in disclosures alongside oil, wheat, cocoa). For mithai/sweets specifically, a named artisanal producer (Akash Sardar, Sreeji Sweets, Varanasi) is quoted stating "sugar and jaggery together make up nearly half of what goes into most mithai" by cost — this is the most concrete sweets-specific cost-share figure found and should be treated as directional trade testimony, not audited data. [localsamosa.com, "Sugar and Jaggery are Both Getting Pricier," 2026 — site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis]
- **FACT** — India's edible-oil import bill is projected to rise ~9% to ₹1.75 lakh crore in the current oil marketing year (Nov 2025-Oct 2026) vs ₹1.61 lakh crore last year, per the Solvent Extractors' Association (SEA) — driven by higher import volumes AND rupee depreciation, not primarily a soybean-specific global price spike. [Business Standard, "India's edible oil import bill may rise 9% to ₹1.75 trn this year: SEA," Jul 22, 2026 — https://www.business-standard.com/economy/news/india-s-edible-oil-import-bill-may-rise-9-to-1-75-trn-this-year-sea-126072201029_1.html]
- **FACT** — India's edible oil imports rose 7% to 103.88 lakh tonnes in Nov 2025-Jun 2026 vs 97.29 lakh tonnes a year earlier; soybean oil prices were up ~14% YoY as of June 2026 even as import *volumes* of soybean oil fell month-on-month (Jun 2026 vs May 2026), and total edible oil imports fell 30% in June 2026 alone. [ChiniMandi, "India's edible oil imports fall 30% in June as palm, soybean oil shipments decline," 2026; search synthesis]

## 2. Price Transmission History

Historical pattern across FMCG snacks/biscuits/soaps is a **three-lever response**: (a) direct MRP hikes on larger/premium packs, (b) grammage/pack-size cuts ("shrinkflation") concentrated at ₹5/₹10/₹20 price points, (c) formulation/sourcing substitution (e.g., palm-soy blending ratios). Concrete historical instances found:

- **FACT** — Palm oil prices rose ~35-40% from mid-September (2024) after an import-duty increase plus a rise in global prices; HUL and Wipro responded by hiking soap prices ~7-8%. [Business Standard, "Leading FMCG players hike soap prices by 7-8% as palm oil rates go up," Nov 29, 2024 — https://www.business-standard.com/amp/industry/news/leading-fmcg-players-hike-soap-prices-by-7-8-as-palm-oil-rates-go-up-124112900896_1.html]
- **FACT** — Palm oil, coffee, cocoa and wheat price surges (palm oil, coffee, cocoa cited as up 50-60% over the prior year in that period) forced FMCG players into either 3-5% MRP hikes or grammage/pack-size cuts to hold ₹-price points, out of fear of volume loss from an outright price hike. [search synthesis of Business Standard/trade press coverage, late 2024]
- **FACT** — Britannia said in an earlier cost-inflation cycle that it expected to add another 1.5-2% of effective pricing in the following quarter through "shrinkflation" specifically in its ₹5 and ₹10 biscuit packs, citing elevated sugar and palm oil costs. [search synthesis, trade press coverage of Britannia commentary]
- **FACT** — Grammage reductions accounted for nearly 65% of the price increases Britannia effected in 2021-2022 — i.e., roughly two-thirds of realized "pricing" in that cycle was invisible pack-size shrinkage rather than a sticker MRP hike. Since ~50-55% of Britannia's sales are in small/low-unit packs, shrinkflation (not overt price hikes) is structurally the default lever below the ~₹20 pack-price threshold; above that, overt MRP increases are used instead. [theprint.in, "You're not imagining it, Maggi to Vim bar—FMCGs are getting smaller. Shrinkflation is here" — site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis]
- **FACT** — Historically, when palm oil cost pressure hit, "volume growth held on despite sharp price increases of about 10%, driven two-thirds by grammage cuts in convenience price-point packs (~40% of Britannia's sales) and one-third from MRP increases" — i.e., the realized "price hike" investors saw was itself already a blend of overt price + shrinkflation, weighted toward shrinkflation. [search synthesis of Business Standard analyst commentary on Britannia, exact period not independently confirmed — flag as needing a firmer date before publication]
- **FACT — current cycle, 2025 festive run-up** — Sweetmakers (mithai) were preparing a **10-15% retail price increase** ahead of Ganpati/Navratri/Diwali 2025, citing sugar, milk, ghee, dry fruit and fuel cost inflation; retail sugar had crossed ₹65/kg in some Maharashtra markets. Concretely, **peda rose from ₹550-560/kg to ₹750-780/kg** — a jump of roughly 35-40%, i.e., mithai-segment pass-through, where it happens at all, is far steeper and more direct than the blended, partially-hidden increases organized biscuit/snack players use. [Goodreturns, "Mithai Prices Set to Rise 10-15% Ahead of Ganpati, Navratri & Diwali; Sugar, Milk Costs Hit Sweetmakers," 2025 — https://www.goodreturns.in/news/mithai-prices-set-to-rise-10-15-ahead-of-ganpati-navratri-diwali-sugar-milk-costs-hit-sweetmakers-1533135.html (site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis)]
- **FACT** — Diwali/Dhanteras 2025 season saw sweets/dry-fruit/snack prices run roughly 20% above the prior year on average, with premium items like kaju katli up to 25% and mid-tier snacks up ~18-19%. [actowizsolutions.com Diwali price-tracking report, 2025 — site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis; treat as a lower-rigor data aggregator, corroborate before using headline number in the article]
- **INFERENCE** — Taken together, the historical pattern strongly suggests that when both edible oil and sugar spike simultaneously (the 2026 setup), organized players will again split the response across grammage cuts (small packs), selective MRP hikes (larger/premium packs and non-magic-price-point SKUs), and oil-source substitution — while pure mithai (no fixed pack-price anchor, sold by weight) has historically gone straight to blunt per-kg price hikes of 10-40%.

## 3. Elasticity / Demand Sensitivity Evidence

- **FACT** — 54% of urban Indians said they planned to spend *more* this Diwali (2025), especially on sweets, clothing and accessories — consistent with festival mithai/gifting demand being materially ritual- and gifting-driven rather than pure discretionary spend. [search synthesis citing a consumer-sentiment survey, 2025]
- **FACT** — Industry/trade commentary states sugar demand in India "typically rises from August to November during major festivals such as Ganesh Chaturthi, Dussehra and Diwali" and that "every year during festivals sugar demand goes up, stockists have raised purchases, and demand will remain robust" even amid the 2026 price spike — i.e., trade-side expectation is that festival sugar/sweets demand is materially inelastic in the short window despite the price shock. [CNBC, "India sugar prices, festival demand," Aug 24, 2026 — https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/24/india-sugar-prices-festival-demand.html (site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis)] Note the CNBC headline itself frames the 2026 rally as partly "speculation, not supply shortage" per an Indian official — a direct counter-voice the article should engage with, not omit.
- **FACT — historical proxy from biscuits, not sweets** — Britannia's own history shows volume growth *held* even through a ~10% effective price increase (achieved mostly via grammage cuts), consistent with biscuits/snacks in India having relatively low price elasticity within a normal single-digit-to-low-double-digit price move, especially when much of the increase is delivered invisibly through grammage rather than sticker price. [search synthesis, Britannia analyst commentary]
- **INFERENCE** — The mithai-specific evidence (peda +35-40%, kaju katli +25%) with continued "record festive sales" expectations (CAIT's ₹4.75 lakh crore festive sales estimate for 2025) suggests **festival-window demand for sweets is more price-inelastic than everyday-snack demand** — gifting/ritual purchases (mithai boxes, prasad, corporate gifting) are less deferrable than an everyday biscuit purchase, which can be down-traded or delayed. However, no source found here isolates *volume* impact specifically for mithai after a 25-40% price hike (all evidence found is about *price and value* of festive spend, not confirmed unit/kg volumes) — this is a genuine open gap; the inelasticity read is a reasonable inference from spend-intent data, not a proven volume result.
- **INFERENCE — gold as an imperfect elasticity analogue** — Gold, another ritual/gifting-heavy festival category, saw volume declines of up to ~15-27% at record prices even as festive buying persisted — a useful cautionary comparator that "ritual purchase" does not equal "zero elasticity." If sweets pricing moves as steeply as mithai already has (25-40%), some volume give-back at the margin (grammage down-trading to smaller sweet boxes, mix-shift to lower-cost sweets, or jaggery-based/ home-made substitution) is plausible even in a low-elasticity category. Treat any claim of "festival sweets demand is fully inelastic" as an overstatement not supported by hard volume data found in this research pass.

## 4. Organized vs Unorganized Segment Impact

- **FACT** — Britannia (organized) explicitly uses forward/hedged palm-oil procurement (covered ~5 months forward per FY26 commentary) — a risk-management tool unavailable to small halwais who buy sugar, milk/khoya and oil on the spot market. [Britannia FY26 commentary, search synthesis]
- **FACT** — Bikanervala (organized, large branded sweets/snacks player) publicly stated in mid-2024 that it would **not** hike sweets prices despite a rise in milk prices, choosing to absorb the cost — a decision only viable for a scaled player with diversified category mix and balance-sheet depth. [Business Standard, "Bikanervala Foods won't hike sweets' prices; to absorb rise in milk prices," Jun 3, 2024 — https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/bikanervala-foods-won-t-hike-sweets-prices-to-absorb-rise-in-milk-prices-124060300725_1.html (site blocked for direct fetch, content via search synthesis)]
- **FACT** — By contrast, the unorganized sector — "hundreds of thousands of small workshops, home-based units and standalone sweet shops (halwai)" — runs production that is "manual, seasonal ... and highly localized," buying fresh milk/khoya, sugar and ghee without forward contracts. A named halwai (Sreeji Sweets, Varanasi) is on record saying "there's no clever recipe tweak that fully absorbs" a simultaneous sugar+jaggery spike, and that operators are choosing between thinner margins or trimming portion sizes rather than reformulating, because jaggery is treated as "sacrosanct" in specific traditional preparations (e.g., ukadiche modak). [localsamosa.com, 2026 — search synthesis, site blocked for direct fetch]
- **FACT** — Peda (a category dominated by unorganized/local sweet shops, unlike packaged barfi/soan papdi from Haldiram's/Bikanervala) saw an outright **35-40% retail price jump** (₹550-560/kg → ₹750-780/kg) in the current cycle — a much larger and more direct pass-through than the blended low-single-digit-to-low-double-digit realized price increases organized biscuit/snack players have taken historically. This is the clearest quantified evidence in this research of the "unorganized bears disproportionate, blunter price risk" leg of the thesis. [Goodreturns, 2025 — search synthesis]
- **INFERENCE** — CRISIL's own framing that "organised FMCG players' steady 8-10% revenue growth to be price-led" alongside a forecast that organized players' gross margins will still fall ~300-350bps in FY27 because "pass-through of cost inflation will be partial amid competitive pressures and risk of downtrading" implies organized players absorb a meaningful share of cost inflation too — they are better positioned, not immune. The thesis's framing of "better positioned to pass through" should be qualified: organized players pass through *faster and via more levers* (grammage, mix, hedging, multi-category cross-subsidy), not necessarily *fully or painlessly*. [CRISIL Ratings press release, May 2026 — https://www.crisilratings.com/en/home/newsroom/press-releases/2026/05/organised-fmcg-players-steady-8-10-percent-revenue-growth-to-be-price-led.html]
- **INFERENCE** — No source found gives a hard number for unorganized-mithai-sector operating margins (pre- or post-shock). The "half of mithai cost is sugar+jaggery" testimony plus the near-total lack of hedging/forward buying strongly implies unorganized margins are both thinner at baseline and more directly exposed to spot-price swings than a listed FMCG company's blended, multi-category, partially-hedged cost base — but this is triangulated from qualitative trade commentary, not disclosed financials, and should be labeled as inference in the article.

## 5. Substitution Economics

- **FACT** — The palm-soybean oil price spread was roughly **$100-150/MT in August 2026**, near the upper end of the range at which palm-soy blending becomes economically attractive; at a $150/MT spread, a 50/50 palm-soybean blend saves roughly $75/MT versus pure soybean oil while keeping a liquid cooking-oil profile. [search synthesis citing procurement/market-intelligence sources (Jit Aree, Chemtradeasia-style trackers), Aug 2026 — treat as trade-intelligence-grade, not primary, data]
- **FACT / caveat** — Substitution is application-limited: for **fried snacks and confectionery, palm oil often has no true substitute** (stability/shelf-life properties), while soybean oil is preferred for liquid/emulsified products — meaning namkeen/snack fryers (the segment most exposed to palm oil, per Section 1) have *less* substitution flexibility than the article's framing of "palm vs soy substitution" might imply; the substitution lever is more available to biscuit/bakery formulations than to fried-snack lines.
- **FACT** — On the sweetener side, jaggery (gur) prices have themselves **doubled to ₹80-90/kg**, moving roughly in tandem with (and partly driven by) the sugar spike, as consumers/producers attempted to substitute toward jaggery and pushed up its price too — i.e., the "obvious" sugar substitute is not currently a cost escape valve; it has already re-priced upward in sympathy. [search synthesis of Odisha Connect / Deccan Herald coverage, 2026]
- **FACT** — For certain traditional preparations (e.g., ukadiche modak for Ganesh Chaturthi), jaggery is treated as non-negotiable/"sacrosanct," so producers report absorbing margin hits or cutting portion sizes rather than reformulating — a real-world case where the thesis's assumed "ingredient substitution" lever is culturally/product-constrained, not freely available. [localsamosa.com, 2026 — search synthesis]
- **INFERENCE** — Net picture: substitution economics offer organized manufacturers a *partial, application-specific* hedge (palm-soy blending in liquid/bakery uses; multi-oil sourcing flexibility; possibly higher use of sugar syrups/alternative sweeteners in packaged confectionery where recipe rigidity is lower) but offer **very limited relief for fried-snack lines and for traditional/ritual sweets**, where either the oil type or the sweetener is functionally fixed by product identity. This cuts against an overly clean "manufacturers will just substitute their way out" reading of the thesis.

## 6. Rough Sensitivity Framing (Explicit Assumptions — Illustrative, Not Company-Specific)

**Purpose:** order-of-magnitude margin-impact framing only, built from the cost-share ranges found above. All inputs are stated explicitly; do not present as precise or audited.

**Shock inputs used (from Sections 1-3, current cycle):**
- Sugar: retail price +36% in 5 weeks / ~+40% in ~2 months to record highs (Jul-Aug 2026); mill-gate sugar +15.6% in one month (Jul 20 → Aug 20, 2026).
- Edible oil: soybean oil +14% YoY (as of Jun 2026); India's total edible-oil import bill +9% YoY (FY basis), driven mostly by rupee depreciation and import volume, not a pure global soy-specific spike.

**Assumption set A — Branded fried-snacks/namkeen producer** (Bikaji/Prataap/Gopal-type):
- Frying oil = 28% of raw-material cost (midpoint of the disclosed "quarter to a third" range).
- Raw material = 70% of COGS; COGS = 70% of net sales (illustrative, not company-specific).
- Oil share of net sales ≈ 0.28 × 0.70 × 0.70 ≈ **13.7% of sales.**
- A 15% oil cost inflation (mid-case, between the 14% YoY soybean move and a higher palm-oil scenario) → cost increase ≈ 0.15 × 13.7% ≈ **~2.1 percentage points of sales**, i.e., a 2-point gross-margin hit *before any price/grammage offset.*
- At a typical listed snack-company gross margin in the 30-35% band, an unmitigated 2-point hit is a **6-7% relative gross-margin erosion** — material, but recoverable via the historical mixed levers (partial MRP hikes + grammage cuts + palm-soy blend optimization where the product allows it).

**Assumption set B — Branded biscuit producer** (Britannia/Parle-type):
- Palm oil = 20% of raw-material cost ("nearly a fifth," per Section 1); sugar assumed at 10% of raw-material cost (assumption — no company disclosed figure found; stated explicitly as an estimate, not sourced).
- Raw material = 65% of COGS; COGS = 65% of sales.
- Combined oil+sugar share of sales ≈ (0.20+0.10) × 0.65 × 0.65 ≈ **12.7% of sales.**
- Applying the two shocks separately — oil +15%, sugar +40% — weighted by their assumed cost shares: (0.20×0.15 + 0.10×0.40) × 0.65 × 0.65 ≈ (0.03+0.04) × 0.4225 ≈ **~3.0 percentage points of sales**, i.e., a larger hit than the pure-snacks case because of sugar's outsized current spike, even though sugar is assumed to be the smaller cost-share item.
- This is broadly consistent with the *direction* of ITC's actually-reported ~700bps gross margin contraction in Q1 FY26 (a different quarter/shock mix, but same order of magnitude), lending the framework some plausibility — though ITC's number is a real disclosed result and this Assumption-B figure is a hypothetical illustration, not a forecast of ITC or Britannia's FY27 number.

**Assumption set C — Unorganized/local mithai maker (halwai):**
- Sugar + jaggery ≈ 45% of total ingredient cost (using the Sreeji Sweets "nearly half" testimony as the anchor).
- Ingredient cost ≈ 75% of total cost (thin fixed-cost base, minimal brand/marketing/hedging overhead vs. an organized player) — assumption, not sourced.
- Sugar/jaggery share of total cost ≈ 0.45 × 0.75 ≈ **33.75% of total cost.**
- A 40% sugar/jaggery cost spike → cost increase ≈ 0.40 × 33.75% ≈ **~13.5 percentage points of total cost** — against a baseline halwai operating margin that trade sources consistently describe as thin (no hard number found, but qualitatively "thin/spot-market/no hedge"). Even a baseline margin in the high single digits to low teens (a plausible but unsourced range for small-format food retail) would be **more than fully consumed** by an unmitigated shock of this size, which is consistent with the observed real-world response: halwais moving straight to a blunt 35-40% retail price hike (peda) rather than absorbing the cost or attempting a formulation workaround.

**What this sensitivity framing does and doesn't show:**
- It shows the qualitative asymmetry is directionally real and roughly an order of magnitude apart (organized: mid-single-digit % of sales at risk, absorbable via multiple levers; unorganized: a shock that can exceed the entire margin, absorbable only via a large, direct price hike or a quality/grammage cut).
- It does **not** constitute a validated financial model — the cost-share assumptions for sugar in biscuits, and for the halwai cost structure, are estimates built to be transparent and falsifiable, not sourced disclosures. The article should present this as "a rough illustration of the mechanism," with assumptions shown, not as a market-sized number.

## Open Gaps

1. **No company discloses a clean "sugar as % of COGS" figure** for biscuits, snacks or packaged sweets. All sugar-cost-share figures in this report are either bundled ("commodity basket") disclosures or single-halwai testimony (Sreeji Sweets' "~half"). A more rigorous number would require an analyst note (e.g., a Kotak/Motilal Oswal/ICICI Securities FMCG sector report) that this search pass did not surface directly — worth a follow-up search specifically for brokerage PDF reports on Britannia/Nestle India/ITC cost-structure breakdowns.
2. **No hard volume/elasticity data for mithai specifically.** All elasticity evidence is either (a) spend-intent survey data (54% plan to spend more) or (b) an imperfect analogue (gold volume decline at record prices). A genuine "did mithai kg-volumes fall after the 25-40% 2025 price hike" data point was not found and should be flagged as unconfirmed if the article implies festival sweets demand is "inelastic."
3. **The sugar deficit narrative has two competing versions in the source base**: (a) ISMA's original Nov 2025 forecast of a large 2025-26 output *increase* (31-34.9 MMT range, framed as surplus-supportive, prompting the government to approve exports), versus (b) the actually-realized ~27.2 MMT (USDA-cut-to-30MMT) outturn with recovery falling to 8.3% vs 10.25% benchmark, which is what produced the Aug 2026 price spike and first sugar imports in a decade. The article should tell this as a **forecast-failure story** (bad early estimates → premature export approvals → scramble in Aug 2026), not simply assert "deficit" as a static fact — this is actually a *stronger*, more specific story than a generic deficit claim, and is well-timed for a Sept 2026 publication date.
4. **The CNBC "speculation, not supply shortage" official pushback** on the Aug 2026 sugar price rally was found only as a headline/snippet; the full argument (and who made it — likely a food ministry or ISMA official) should be tracked down and represented fairly, since it directly complicates a simple "deficit" framing the thesis assumes.
5. **Palm oil's own price trajectory in 2026** is less clearly evidenced here than soybean oil's; most of the "oil pressure" evidence found is either (a) soybean-specific (+14% YoY) or (b) about the rupee-driven import *bill*, not the underlying commodity price. A dedicated search on Malaysian/Indonesian palm oil FOB price trends through 2026 (MPOB data, Bursa Malaysia futures) would sharpen this leg.
6. **No disclosed operating-margin figure for any unorganized halwai/mithai business** — by definition, most are unregistered/informal, so this gap may be structural rather than a research miss. The sensitivity framing in Section 6 (Assumption Set C) should be clearly labeled as an illustrative construction, not a sourced number, in the final article.
7. **Duty-free U.S. soybean oil access** (from a trade-framework agreement referenced in search results) could be a *margin-relief* wildcard for FY27 that cuts against the thesis's "soybean oil price/supply pressure" framing — worth a dedicated follow-up on the India-U.S. trade deal terms and timing relative to the 2026 festival window.
